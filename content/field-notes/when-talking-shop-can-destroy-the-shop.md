*The credentials that impress your colleagues might be the very things making your next client nervous.*

I once told a prospective client I'd already closed about 200 transactions that year.

I thought I was establishing credibility.

She thought I was telling her I had absolutely no time for her.

And you know what?

She wasn't wrong.

In my head, I was saying, *You're in very capable hands. I've done this hundreds of times. I've seen the problems. I know how to handle them.*

Apparently, what she heard was, *Please take a number. Your estimated wait time is three to five business days.*

Same conversation. Two completely different interpretations.

That experience taught me something I wish I'd understood much earlier in my career.

**What impresses some people can terrify others.**

And nowhere is that more obvious than in an industry where we've become exceptionally good at talking about how successful we are.

## We love talking shop

Real estate professionals have an entire language dedicated to explaining their importance to one another.

Fifty million in sales volume.

Seventy-two closings.

Top one percent.

Number-one team.

Three hundred units.

Chairman's Platinum Double-Diamond Whatever.

There are more number-one agents in Miami than there are neighborhoods.

And I'm not questioning the accomplishments. Building a successful real estate business takes considerable work, and people deserve recognition for what they've achieved.

But here's where we get ourselves into trouble.

We're so accustomed to speaking to other industry professionals that we forget the person sitting across the table may have absolutely no idea what those numbers mean.

An agent hears 200 transactions and thinks experience.

A broker hears 200 transactions and thinks production.

A lender hears 200 transactions and might see an opportunity for a valuable professional relationship.

A prospective client hears 200 transactions and wonders whether you'll remember her name.

She isn't necessarily impressed.

She's doing math.

Two hundred clients?

How many people are calling you?

Who's answering the phone?

Am I actually going to work with you?

Or am I about to become transaction number 201?

Perfectly reasonable questions.

We sell volume as proof of competence. The consumer can hear volume as proof of neglect.

## Your résumé isn't the client's question

I've spent more than two decades inside South Florida real estate, working across lending, title, contracts, operations, and transaction management.

I've supported thousands of closings, and today I manage transactions for producing agents.

Those credentials mean something to me because I know what it takes to acquire them.

But the person hiring me doesn't necessarily understand that world.

And frankly, why should they?

They're trying to determine whether I can help them.

Not whether I qualify for an award at an industry luncheon.

When I told that prospective client about my production, I gave her information about myself.

I hadn't explained what any of it meant for her.

Two hundred transactions meant I'd developed pattern recognition. I'd encountered financing complications, difficult negotiations, shifting deadlines, missing documents, last-minute surprises, and enough unusual situations to fill a reasonably entertaining book.

It meant I had experience recognizing problems before they became expensive.

It also meant I'd learned the importance of building systems so the work didn't depend on remembering everything myself.

But I didn't say any of that.

I gave her a number and expected her to reach the conclusion I wanted.

She reached a different one.

**Credentials only work if the person hearing them understands what they mean for them.**

Which seems obvious.

It wasn't obvious to me that day.

## We've been taught that looking unavailable makes us desirable

There is a peculiar belief in professional services that the busier you appear, the more successful people will assume you are.

The calendar is packed.

The phone never stops ringing.

Another closing. Another listing. Another award.

Sorry, can't talk. Back-to-back appointments.

Perhaps somewhere along the way, we decided that being difficult to reach was a form of social proof.

There's a lie in real estate that if you can make yourself look sufficiently unavailable, people will desperately want to hire you.

That's dating advice.

I'm not convinced it's a client-service strategy.

Of course, clients want someone experienced. They want confidence. They want evidence that you're capable of handling an important transaction.

But they also want access to the professional they're trusting with one of the largest financial decisions of their lives.

And high production introduces a question that a lot of professionals forget to answer.

*Who am I actually hiring?*

You?

Your assistant?

Your buyer's agent?

Your showing assistant?

Somebody called Kyle whom I've never met?

I hired Beyoncé and somehow I'm on tour with the backup dancers.

Now, there's nothing wrong with having a team. Some of the best-run businesses I've encountered depend on excellent support staff and clearly defined responsibilities.

The problem comes when the client discovers the arrangement after they've already committed.

A strong team can mean better coverage, faster communication, and more consistent service.

But only if the client understands who does what, who remains accountable, and how they can get help when they need it.

Otherwise, the team isn't reassuring.

It's another source of uncertainty.

## Sometimes the less impressive résumé wins

I've watched newer agents do exceptionally well with clients despite competing against professionals who had far more production, larger teams, and better-established names.

Not because the newer agent pretended to have experience they didn't.

Because they were prepared.

They listened.

They answered questions.

They followed through.

They made the person sitting across from them feel like a priority.

There is nothing wrong with being hungry when you're competent.

And availability is not evidence that you're unsuccessful. Sometimes it's your competitive advantage.

I think this gets overlooked because we spend so much time teaching professionals how to establish authority.

Talk about your results.

Mention your awards.

Show your production.

Demonstrate expertise.

Fine.

But while you're busy explaining why you're impressive, someone else might be explaining exactly how they're going to take care of the client.

Guess which conversation is more useful to the person making the decision.

The most successful agent isn't automatically the right agent for every client.

Neither is the newest.

The question is whether the professional's capabilities, service model, and communication style fit what that particular client needs.

A client who wants substantial personal access may prefer a different arrangement from someone who values the infrastructure and coverage of a large team.

Neither preference is irrational.

But neither client should have to guess what they're getting.

## Learn to translate

I still believe accomplishments matter.

I wouldn't tell anyone to hide their experience, apologize for their success, or pretend their production isn't meaningful.

But I've changed how I think about presenting those credentials.

If you've closed a hundred transactions, that isn't just a number.

It may mean you've encountered enough complications to recognize warning signs earlier.

If you've worked in a neighborhood for fifteen years, your value isn't simply that you've been around.

It's that you may understand the local market, properties, relationships, and history beyond what's visible in today's listing data.

If you have a team, the benefit isn't that you've assembled an impressive organizational chart.

It's that your clients may receive better coverage, provided the responsibilities are clear.

If you've built systems, your clients don't need a lecture on your software stack.

They need to understand how those systems help prevent things from getting overlooked.

And if you've managed hundreds of transactions, explain how your business is structured to provide consistent attention rather than assuming the number speaks for itself.

Never make the client translate your credentials into a benefit.

Do that part for them.

There's a simple question I wish I'd asked myself before that conversation years ago.

*Which means for my client… what?*

I've been doing this for twenty years.

Which means for my client…?

I've closed two hundred transactions.

Which means for my client…?

I have a team of ten.

Which means for my client…?

If you can't finish the sentence with something that genuinely benefits the person hiring you, the credential may be more about you than the client.

And that's useful information to have before your next sales appointment.

## The conversation I remember

I don't remember every prospective client I've spoken with over the years.

But I remember that one.

Because I walked into the conversation believing I had an incredibly strong argument for why she should hire me.

I had experience.

I had production.

I had proof that I knew what I was doing.

And without intending to, I gave her a reason to question whether she'd receive the attention she needed.

The fact was true.

My interpretation of what it communicated was incomplete.

That's what talking shop can do.

We get comfortable using the language of our industry, surrounded by people who understand our accomplishments, and forget that the person on the other side of the table has a completely different set of concerns.

I thought she was shopping for my résumé.

She was deciding whether I would take care of her.

And if there's one thing I took from that conversation, it's this:

**The most important part of a credential isn't what it says about you. It's what it means for the person trusting you.**

Sometimes the smartest thing you can do with an impressive number is stop talking long enough to explain why it matters.
