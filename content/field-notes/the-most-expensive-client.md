I've watched agents work harder to keep one difficult client than they worked to get the client in the first place. Sometimes that client came from an internet inquiry. No previous relationship, no referral, no history together. But somewhere along the way, the client figured out something important: the agent was terrified of losing the deal.

And once that happens, the negotiations get interesting.

First, the agent is asked to reduce their commission. Then the title company is asked to lower its fees. The lender gets a phone call. The seller's agent is approached for a credit. Apparently, we've entered a transaction where everybody is expected to contribute to the client's personal savings account.

There's usually an ultimatum attached: *"If you don't do this, I'm canceling."*

Now, negotiation is part of real estate. Buyers should advocate for themselves, and sometimes there are perfectly legitimate reasons to renegotiate or walk away. That's not what I'm talking about. I'm talking about a client who uses cancellation as a negotiating strategy with practically everyone involved.

Sometimes it doesn't work. But when it does, something interesting happens: nothing gets better.

The client is still unhappy, still making demands, still contacting people directly, and still convinced that everyone else is doing something wrong. Meanwhile, the agent is spending most of the transaction trying to prevent a cancellation, while other professionals quietly pick up responsibilities that were never theirs to begin with.

And that's where the cost becomes much bigger than the commission somebody agreed to give up.

## There's a Fine Line Between an Exception and a Pattern

I've worked with enough lenders, title companies, attorneys, and agents to know that most professionals understand when a transaction gets complicated. Things happen. Clients become unreasonable. Deals hit unexpected problems. Nobody expects every closing to be a perfectly choreographed performance.

But there's a difference between asking someone to help you through an unusual situation and repeatedly expecting them to absorb the consequences of a client you cannot manage.

The first time, people make an exception. The second time, they remember the first. Eventually, there's a pattern.

Nobody sends an email announcing that they're reconsidering their professional relationship with you. The damage is usually quieter than that. Calls don't get returned quite as quickly. The willingness to accommodate another unusual request starts disappearing. People become less interested in getting involved.

And really, why should someone else's business take a financial hit because an agent hasn't developed the skills to manage a difficult client?

An agent can walk away from one of these transactions believing they did an extraordinary job because the deal closed. But closing a transaction and protecting the relationships that support your business are not necessarily the same accomplishment.

## Know the Game, the Players, and the Rules

I believe the line should be drawn when negotiation becomes manipulation.

And an agent needs to recognize that moment without confusing every objection or request for a concession with bad behavior.

One warning sign is when the client begins treating the agent's commission as an untapped treasure chest of gold coins available for whatever new demand appears that afternoon.

Commission is negotiable. That doesn't mean it's an emergency fund for every objection in the transaction.

But setting boundaries requires something more substantial than confidence. You need to know your paperwork. You need to understand what was agreed to, what the contract provides, which obligations belong to which parties, and what options actually exist when a dispute arises.

This is where some agents put themselves at a disadvantage. If your agreements are incomplete, your documentation is disorganized, or you haven't kept up with your own responsibilities, it's difficult to have a credible conversation about contractual rights and obligations.

You cannot effectively explain the rules if you're still trying to figure out what they are.

And when an agent doesn't have the skills to manage the situation, the responsibility often spills onto everybody else: the lender, title company, attorney, other agent, and whoever happens to answer the phone.

## The Cost Nobody Puts on the Closing Statement

I understand why an agent might fight to preserve a deal. Income matters. Pipelines fluctuate. There are bills to pay and months when another commission makes a significant difference.

But professional relationships have value, too.

The people who answer your calls, share information, solve legitimate problems, and help you navigate complicated transactions are part of the infrastructure of your business. You build those relationships over years, often one closing at a time.

Every unnecessary exception spends a little of that goodwill. Every request that someone else absorb another expense spends a little more.

And the most frustrating part is that the agent may never realize what happened. The transaction closed. The client got their concession. Everybody moved on.

Except the next time the agent needs help, the reception is a little different.

Not necessarily because someone is holding a grudge. Because they've learned what working with that agent might cost them.

The goal isn't to win every argument with a client. It's to understand the difference between advocating for someone and allowing their behavior to compromise the professional relationships your business depends on.

Sometimes the most expensive transaction isn't the one that falls apart.

It's the one that closes, while quietly costing you relationships that took years to build.
