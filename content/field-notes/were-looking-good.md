There's a sentence I hear in real estate transactions that tells me almost nothing while somehow managing to sound reassuring.

*"We're looking good."*

Excellent. Looking good at what?

Has underwriting reviewed the file? Have the outstanding conditions been satisfied? Is the appraisal complete? Has the lender issued the required approval? Are we waiting on documentation from the borrower, or has someone simply had a positive feeling about the transaction this morning?

Because those are very different situations, and they can all apparently be described as looking good.

After thousands of financed transactions, I've learned that positive language and meaningful information are not necessarily the same thing.

And optimism, while pleasant, is difficult to put on a closing calendar.

## A Feeling Is Not a Milestone

I understand why people want to keep everyone comfortable during a transaction. Buying a property is stressful. Agents need to keep clients informed, lenders are managing numerous moving parts, and nobody enjoys delivering news that might cause concern.

But reassurance becomes a problem when it substitutes for information.

If I ask where a loan stands, I'm not looking for a general assessment of the team's emotional condition.

I'm looking for something concrete.

Has the file been reviewed? What remains outstanding? Who is responsible for providing it? Is there a realistic timeline for the next approval milestone? And has anything happened that might affect a contractual deadline or the expected closing?

Those questions aren't an accusation. They're the information needed to manage a transaction responsibly.

Of course, not every lender can predict exactly when underwriting will finish or guarantee a closing date. Conditions can change, and new issues can appear.

That's precisely why accurate status updates matter.

There's a meaningful difference between saying, "We're waiting on underwriting," and explaining that the file has been submitted, which conditions remain, and when another update is expected.

One gives people a general feeling. The other gives them something they can plan around.

## The Problem With Answers That Sound Like Answers

This isn't exclusive to lenders.

Real estate has an impressive collection of responses that sound cooperative without resolving the question.

You send an email asking whether the deposit was received, whether the application was submitted, and whether the buyer signed the outstanding documents.

The reply comes back:

*"Received."*

Received what? The email? The information? A sudden calling from God?

We still have three unanswered questions.

And sometimes the person responding genuinely believes they've communicated effectively. They acknowledged the message, offered reassurance, or confirmed they're working on something.

But communication isn't complete simply because someone replied.

The point is to exchange the information necessary for the next person to do their job.

If the next person has to ask the same question again, the exchange probably wasn't as useful as it could have been.

## Clarity Is a Form of Professional Courtesy

A transaction involves people working from different offices, systems, schedules, and professional responsibilities. The agent is communicating with a client. The lender is managing underwriting requirements. The title company is handling its work. The transaction manager is monitoring documentation, deadlines, and the movement of information.

Nobody has the complete picture automatically.

That picture has to be assembled through communication.

And when the answers are vague, the cost isn't limited to a few extra emails. People begin making assumptions. Follow-ups multiply. Risks become harder to evaluate, and the distinction between something that's delayed and something that's simply unconfirmed starts to disappear.

I don't expect every update to come with a detailed report. Sometimes a brief message is perfectly sufficient.

But a useful update should tell the recipient what has happened, what remains, or when they'll know more.

Even "I don't have confirmation yet; I've followed up and expect an answer tomorrow" is more valuable than a confident statement that everything looks wonderful.

At least everyone knows where things stand.

Over the years, I've become less interested in how reassuring a status update sounds and more interested in whether I can do something with the information.

Because when a deadline is approaching, a positive attitude is appreciated.

An actual answer is considerably more useful.

And "we're looking good" is still a weather forecast with no location.
