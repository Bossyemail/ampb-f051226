Real estate agents sometimes ask what they are actually delegating when they hire a transaction coordinator. The question is fair. The role is common enough that most agents have worked with one, but the scope still varies widely depending on the person, the brokerage, and the transaction.

This article describes how TC work is structured here — what falls inside the scope of coordination and what does not.

## What a TC manages

Transaction coordination covers the administrative and deadline-tracking work that begins once a contract is fully executed and continues through closing.

In practice that includes:

- Reviewing the contract and addenda for completeness and flagging any missing items
- Building and maintaining the transaction file
- Tracking critical dates: inspection period, financing contingency, appraisal, walk-through, closing
- Confirming receipt of the deposit and following up when it has not been received
- Distributing fully executed documents to all parties
- Coordinating with the lender, title company, inspectors, and — where applicable — condominium or homeowners associations
- Preparing and sending written communications that document key steps in the transaction
- Following up systematically to keep all parties moving toward closing

The goal is a transaction file that is complete, a timeline that is tracked, and communication that is documented.

## What a TC does not do

Transaction coordination does not replace the agent, the lender, or the title company.

The agent remains responsible for negotiations, client advice, strategy, and all decisions that require their license or professional judgment. If a buyer wants to waive an inspection contingency or negotiate a repair credit, that decision belongs to the agent and the client — not the TC.

Lender underwriting, loan approval, and rate lock decisions are the lender's domain. The TC coordinates communication with the lender but has no authority or involvement in the loan itself.

Title examination, closing preparation, settlement statements, and disbursements remain with the title company or closing attorney. The TC does not prepare the HUD or settlement statement and does not direct the closing table.

## Why the distinction matters

The clearest transactions are ones where every professional understands their lane. When a TC steps outside coordination and into advisory territory — or when an agent assumes the TC is managing something they are not — things fall through the gaps.

Working with a TC is most effective when the agent stays engaged with the client, the negotiations, and the decisions that require their license, while delegating the administrative tracking and documentation to the TC.

That division of labor is what makes the relationship work.
