*From the first lead to the final commission check, real estate agents carry costs, risks, and responsibilities that rarely make it into the closing announcement.*

Real estate may be the only industry where someone can announce a $10 million year and still be wondering whether to put their next brokerage invoice on a credit card.

Ten million in sales.

Beautiful number.

Very impressive on a graphic.

Unfortunately, the mortgage company has expressed absolutely no interest in accepting production volume as payment.

And this is where things get interesting.

Because there's an entire business operating behind that closing announcement. One that starts long before somebody signs a contract and continues long after the congratulatory photograph.

Everybody wants to know how much you sold.

Almost nobody asks what it took to get there, what you actually earned, or how much you kept.

## First, you have to find someone who might actually buy something

Before there's a closing, there's a lead.

And before there's a lead, there's usually money going somewhere.

Advertising. Marketing. Photography. Video. Social media. Networking events. Open houses. Websites. Software. CRM systems. Signs. Postcards. Database subscriptions.

Or time. An extraordinary amount of time.

You might spend months building relationships before someone even considers buying or selling. You answer questions, explain neighborhoods, discuss pricing, send listings, follow up, remember birthdays, and occasionally become an unpaid therapist for someone who isn't quite ready to move.

Some leads convert quickly.

Others have been "thinking about buying next year" since 2018.

You keep showing up because that's how relationships work, and because there is no magical machine that deposits qualified buyers into your calendar every morning.

Unless someone has invented one.

In which case, please send the link.

## Then you nurture the relationship. For free.

Let's say the lead is promising.

Wonderful.

Now comes the part where you invest even more time without any certainty of getting paid.

For buyers, there are conversations about financing, neighborhoods, budgets, expectations, property searches, showings, offers, negotiations, and decisions that can change approximately seventeen times before lunch.

For sellers, there are pricing conversations, property preparation, photography, listing appointments, marketing, showings, feedback, price adjustments, and the delicate art of explaining that the house down the street selling for an extraordinary amount doesn't automatically mean theirs will.

You can do everything professionally and still not get the business.

The buyer might decide to renew their lease.

The seller might decide they love their house after all.

Someone's cousin might suddenly become a real estate agent.

Congratulations to the cousin.

Your investment of time doesn't come with a refund.

That's the first uncomfortable number in this business: the cost of opportunities that never become transactions.

And those costs have to be supported by the transactions that do close.

## Congratulations. You're under contract.

Now the work begins again.

There's the effective date, deposits, inspections, financing, appraisal, title, association requirements where applicable, insurance, scheduling, communication, walkthrough, and closing.

The contract has deadlines. The buyer has concerns. The seller has expectations. The lender has conditions. The title company has questions.

And everyone would like an update.

Preferably yesterday.

This is the part of the business I know particularly well.

After more than 3,000 closings, I can tell you that most transactions don't get into trouble because of one spectacular mistake.

They get there through assumptions, delays, unanswered questions, and people discovering information a little later than they should have.

The agent may have done an excellent job generating the lead, building the relationship, negotiating the agreement, and getting the contract signed.

But the transaction still has to survive everything between that signature and the closing table.

And the agent doesn't control all of it.

Not even close.

## The things you can't control still affect your paycheck

You don't control interest rates.

You don't control whether your buyer wakes up one morning and decides homeownership suddenly feels like an unnecessary commitment.

You don't control what an inspection uncovers behind a wall.

You don't control whether a seller has a change of heart, whether a lender discovers another condition, or whether an association takes longer than expected to process an application.

You don't control every appraisal outcome, insurance complication, or financing decision.

And you certainly don't control the buyer who decides that ten days before closing is the perfect time to finance an entire living room.

Because apparently the house needs a sectional before it needs a mortgage approval.

Now, to be fair, not every furniture purchase will jeopardize a loan. But taking on new debt before closing can affect a buyer's financing, and lenders may need to reevaluate the file.

Imagine getting through months of negotiations, inspections, documents, and deadlines only to discover that a sofa has entered the chat.

The agent can educate the client. Set expectations. Explain the risks. Encourage communication with the lender before any major financial changes.

But the agent cannot personally make every decision on the client's behalf.

And that's the problem with measuring this business as though effort automatically produces income.

Sometimes you do the work correctly and the transaction still doesn't close.

Sometimes the client changes their mind.

Sometimes circumstances change.

Sometimes the deal falls apart through no single person's negligence.

And when that happens, the agent may have spent weeks or months working toward a commission that never arrives.

## Now let's assume the deal actually closes

The buyer gets the keys.

The seller gets their proceeds.

Everyone takes a photograph.

Someone posts "JUST SOLD," preferably in capital letters, because apparently the closing doesn't count unless the internet hears about it.

And finally, the commission is earned and paid according to the applicable agreements.

Except the amount associated with the sale isn't necessarily the amount the agent takes home.

From the commission allocated to the agent's side, there may be a brokerage split, referral fee, transaction fee, team split, or other contractual expenses.

Then there's what it cost to generate and service the transaction.

Marketing. Photography. Video. Technology. Transportation. Licensing. Association dues. Continuing education. Insurance. Assistants. Transaction coordination. Office expenses.

Some costs are tied to that particular transaction. Others continue every month whether anything closes or not.

And taxes.

The government, you may have noticed, remains remarkably committed to its business model.

By the time everything is accounted for, the number on the closing graphic and the amount the agent actually retains may be very different.

Which doesn't necessarily mean the agent did anything wrong.

It means gross production isn't profit.

And commissions aren't take-home pay.

## The transactions that didn't close are still on the books

Here's where the math gets particularly unpleasant.

The commission from a successful closing doesn't exist in isolation.

It also has to help pay for the leads that never responded, the appointments that went nowhere, the buyers who never purchased, and the listings that expired.

The marketing campaigns that didn't perform.

The open houses nobody attended.

The properties you showed six times before the buyer decided to wait until next year.

The transactions that collapsed after the inspection.

The deals that fell apart days before closing.

Not every unsuccessful opportunity is a failure. Some clients make perfectly reasonable decisions not to proceed, and some transactions absolutely should be canceled when the circumstances warrant it.

But the time and money invested are still real.

That's why I find it so interesting when people discuss an agent's commission as though someone walked into an office, signed one document, and collected a check.

Sometimes a closing represents months of work.

Sometimes it represents years of relationship-building.

And sometimes it's helping pay for a whole collection of transactions that never made it to the finish line.

The commission is visible.

The cost of earning it usually isn't.

## And don't get me started on looking successful

Real estate is an image-conscious industry. I understand that.

Beautiful homes. Beautiful lifestyles. Beautiful marketing.

And because your business depends on trust, professional presentation matters.

But there's a difference between investing in how you present your business and financing the appearance of a business you haven't actually built.

The luxury car.

The luxury wardrobe.

The luxury photography.

The luxury branding.

The luxury everything, including the monthly payments.

At some point, your business starts supporting the image of your business instead of supporting you.

Buy the car if you can afford it. Wear the watch. Take the vacation.

I genuinely hope you enjoy every dollar you've earned.

I just hope you've calculated what the business actually needs before Instagram congratulates you for spending it.

Because the market doesn't care what your lifestyle costs.

And the next transaction doesn't owe you a commission because the last one was difficult.

## The number that matters after everyone gets paid

I would love to see an annual real estate awards ceremony recognizing the people who built the healthiest businesses.

The agent who increased profit without increasing working hours.

The agent who built a referral network that consistently generates business.

The agent who learned which expenses actually produce a return.

The agent who stopped confusing activity with productivity.

The agent who built enough financial stability to survive a slow quarter without immediately panicking.

The agent who took two weeks off and returned to a business that hadn't caught fire.

Because what I respect most about a successful business isn't how impressive it looks during a good month.

It's whether it can survive a difficult one.

Can you weather a cancellation?

Can you turn down a client who isn't a good fit?

Can you make an investment because the numbers support it rather than because everyone else seems to be doing it?

Can you build a business that supports your life instead of requiring your entire life to support the business?

Those are the calculations worth making.

Real estate agents carry the cost of finding clients, earning trust, managing uncertainty, getting transactions closed, and paying the expenses that keep the operation running.

Sometimes all of that work results in a commission.

Sometimes it doesn't.

So celebrate the closing. You earned that moment.

Take the photograph.

Post the beautiful graphic.

Just remember that the number on the graphic is the beginning of the conversation, not the end.

Because the brutal math of this business isn't how much you sold.

It's everything it took to get paid—and what's left when everyone else has been paid, too.
