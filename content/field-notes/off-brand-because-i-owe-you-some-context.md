*I have a few opinions about money, business, success, and the strange things people will do to look like they're winning. Some of them may explain why I don't always sound like someone who's spent 22 years in real estate.*

I think a lot of people are building lives they don't even want because they're copying what success is supposed to look like.

Bigger house. Newer car. More clients. Bigger team. More income streams. More followers. More everything.

And somehow, less freedom.

I see it in business all the time. Someone reaches a milestone they spent years chasing, celebrates for approximately eleven minutes, and immediately discovers the next thing they're supposed to want.

Achievements have a funny way of reminding you that the peak of one mountain is usually the base of the next.

And if you're not enjoying the climb, at some point you have to wonder what you're doing all this hiking for.

Congratulations on your successful business. Have you considered turning it into a company with fifty employees and a human resources department?

Perhaps you'd also like a podcast.

And a mastermind.

And seven passive income streams requiring your active participation every waking hour.

Wonderful. You must be exhausted.

I have a few opinions about money, business, success, and what constitutes a life well lived. Some might sound strange coming from someone who's spent twenty-two years in real estate.

**So before I give you my two cents, I probably owe you some context.**

## A little about the woman making these observations

I'm Cuban-American, based in Miami, with an amazing Nicaraguan wife and a son who seems convinced every time I press Enter, another hundred dollars appears in my bank account.

One day, I hope to become the rich woman he already thinks I am.

I was raised by a single mother who worked two jobs.

I wasn't daddy's little princess. I was mommy's little warrior.

And while neither childhood nor family structure determines what someone can become, that particular upbringing gave me a fairly specific understanding of responsibility.

I watched a woman do what needed to be done, often without the time, money, or support that would have made it easier.

It taught me that circumstances don't always become convenient before life expects something from you.

It also taught me something I've carried into adulthood: nobody is necessarily coming to save the day.

I work accordingly.

I work like someone who wants an amazing life and has absolutely no intention of asking somebody else to foot the bill.

Because to me, money represents something much more interesting than expensive possessions.

Options.

Freedom.

The ability to make decisions without asking permission from a bank account that's already been promised to six other people.

Especially in the world we live in, where circumstances can change faster than most of us can comfortably adapt.

I drive a paid-off 2017 Hyundai Tucson. It starts, the air conditioning works, and Bluetooth connects. I'm emotionally unavailable to the dealership.

I also own a modest, roughly 972-square-foot lakefront townhome that I consider my fallback and fortress of solitude.

None of this is particularly impressive by Miami standards.

That's fine.

I don't need every part of my life to look impressive.

What I need is enough room to make decisions without feeling financially cornered every time something doesn't go according to plan.

Low fixed expenses give me options. They let me invest, experiment, build software, survive slower periods, and spend twenty days working on an idea that might never make a dollar.

I've learned that the boring stuff finances the interesting stuff.

I'm not trying to look rich.

I'm trying to buy optionality.

## I learned about business from the part nobody photographs

Real estate is almost the only professional world I've ever known.

Before I entered the industry, I worked the ventanita at a Cuban restaurant.

It didn't take long to realize that the money I was making might not even cover the tolls from commuting back and forth to work.

An impressive business model, provided I intended to finance the privilege of being employed.

That experience gave me a very practical reason to find another way forward.

At eighteen, I entered real estate surrounded by professionals who seemed substantially older, more experienced, and better connected than I was.

For years, I worked behind the scenes across lending, title, contracts, compliance, operations, and closings.

I made other people a lot of money.

Some of those years, I was overworked and underpaid. I was also paying attention.

I learned what businesses look like when nobody is watching.

What happens when the client leaves the office. How decisions get made. Who understands the numbers. Who takes responsibility when things go wrong. Who has systems and who has an impressive collection of emergencies.

Before AI came along to help everybody write emails, I was the person people called when they needed to compose a difficult message, think through a complicated situation, or figure out what needed to happen next.

I didn't have a fancy name for it.

I was just the person who could usually make sense of the mess.

My brother-in-law still calls me Google.

Which is lovely, although now that I think about it, he hasn't taken me fishing since 2019.

I'm beginning to suspect the relationship has become somewhat one-sided.

Today, I manage real estate transactions for producing South Florida agents and build software around the operational problems I encounter.

So when I talk about business, I'm not approaching it as an abstract motivational exercise.

I'm inside the thing.

And after more than two decades, I'm learning something new: how to step in front of the camera and share what I know.

I'd much rather build a system than film myself explaining one.

I code for fun. I build three-dimensional wooden puzzles. I enjoy research.

Yes, I realize this may answer several questions you weren't asking.

But I love learning from people who are actually doing the work.

Give me someone with scars from last Tuesday, not just a story about how they did things in 2007.

Experience matters.

So does remaining curious.

## The lens I see things through

I tell you these things because context matters.

Where I come from shapes how I think about money. What I've spent twenty-two years doing shapes how I think about business. And the life I want to build shapes the decisions I'm willing to make along the way.

That's my lens.

I don't presume to know what freedom, success, or happiness should look like for you. Your circumstances, responsibilities, ambitions, and priorities may be entirely different from mine.

And that's precisely the point.

When I question the need for a bigger business, I'm not saying ambition is bad. When I talk about driving an older car, I'm not suggesting everyone should make the same choice. And when I say I want to spend three months in Paris, I'm not proposing that a European café is the universal definition of happiness.

Although I remain open to discussing that over coffee.

I'm sharing what I've learned from the life I've lived, the businesses I've worked inside, the mistakes I've made, and the future I'm trying to build.

Some of my opinions may resonate. Others may not.

You're welcome to disagree. I might even change my mind if you make a convincing argument.

But at least you'll understand where my two cents are coming from.

## I'd rather look boring than be financially fragile

One of the things real estate teaches you is that income and wealth are not interchangeable.

I've seen enough financial arrangements to understand that a large income can coexist with enormous obligations and very little room for error.

Expensive homes. Expensive cars. Expensive lifestyles. Every dollar already assigned to a payment before it arrives.

There's nothing inherently wrong with having expensive things.

But I find it strange when people assume that because someone can afford the monthly payment, they've achieved financial freedom.

Sometimes they've simply achieved a more sophisticated collection of bills.

I have no interest in maintaining a lifestyle that requires every month to be my best month.

I'd rather have reserves.

I'd rather have choices.

I'd rather be able to say no to work that isn't a good fit, experiment with something new, and survive a disappointing quarter without immediately having to reconsider my entire existence.

That may look boring.

Boring is underrated.

Especially when the economy decides to develop a personality.

## "Must be nice" is a terrible way to study money

Working in real estate also gives you a fascinating view into how people accumulate, protect, and sometimes lose wealth.

You see leverage, equity, ownership, risk, timing, and decisions that don't always make sense until years later.

And I hear people respond to someone else's success with, "Must be nice."

Well, yes.

I imagine it probably is.

But I've always found curiosity more useful than resentment.

Sometimes people inherit money. Sometimes they're lucky. Sometimes they were born with advantages most of us never had.

Pretending those things don't exist would be ridiculous.

But sometimes there's also a system behind what you're seeing.

A decision.

A calculated risk.

A long period of patience.

A business model worth studying.

Instead of stopping at "must be nice," I want to understand how something works.

Not so I can copy somebody else's life.

So I can decide whether there's something worth learning from it.

## You probably don't need seven income streams

Somewhere along the way, the internet decided one source of income was a personal failure.

Apparently, you need a rental property, an online course, affiliate links, a software company, an Etsy store, a consulting business, and possibly a vending machine.

You should also start a newsletter.

I'm not entirely sure when you're supposed to sleep.

At some point, you're not diversified.

You're just tired.

I understand the appeal of creating multiple sources of income. I invest, and I build things outside my service business. I don't believe your financial future has to depend on one paycheck or one client.

But diversification and distraction are different things.

Sometimes the most financially useful decision is to get better at what you already do.

Increase your value.

Improve your margins.

Build repeatable systems.

Create enough stability to fund your next idea without making your current business collapse from neglect.

Fragmentation can keep you mediocre at five things instead of excellent at one.

And I say that as someone who can become deeply fascinated by a new project while the previous project is sitting quietly in the corner, wondering whether we've broken up.

## Not every business needs to become an empire

I find this idea particularly strange in real estate.

Someone builds a successful service business.

They earn good money. Their clients like them. They have control over their schedules.

Naturally, the next recommendation is that they should hire twenty people, launch a training program, open an office, and turn their relatively peaceful operation into an organizational structure requiring quarterly meetings.

Why?

Because it could scale?

So can a headache.

There's nothing wrong with building a large business. Some people are excellent at it, and genuinely enjoy leading teams, managing complexity, and taking on that level of responsibility.

But there are also excellent businesses that remain small because that's what their owners actually want.

And not everybody should become an entrepreneur.

Some people would be wealthier, happier, and less stressed as highly paid employees with excellent benefits and a life that doesn't require checking business notifications on vacation.

Entrepreneurship isn't automatically freedom.

Sometimes it's uncertainty with better branding.

The goal shouldn't be to collect business models like souvenirs.

It should be to build something that serves the life you intend to live.

## Busy stopped impressing me a long time ago

I know people who are busy every minute of every day.

Every call is urgent. Every client is difficult. Every Tuesday is a catastrophe.

They are always overwhelmed, always needed, always explaining how nobody can possibly understand the amount of work they have.

I used to find that impressive.

Now I wonder what's wrong with the system.

Sometimes high demand creates legitimate pressure. Sometimes the work really is difficult, and circumstances are genuinely outside anyone's control.

But constant chaos can also come from poor delegation, weak boundaries, unclear processes, or building a business that requires too much personal intervention.

If every Tuesday is an emergency, Tuesday probably isn't the problem.

Competence often looks boring.

Organized files. Clear communication. Reasonable expectations. People who do what they said they would do.

Not particularly cinematic.

Very effective.

I've become suspicious of people selling freedom while working fourteen-hour days to sell you freedom.

And I no longer admire someone simply because they're overwhelmed.

I want to know whether the work produces the outcome they're supposedly doing it for.

## Please don't climb a ladder you don't want to reach the top of

One of the more interesting things about real estate is how many different careers exist inside the same industry.

You can be an agent, a broker, a team leader, an investor, a developer, an operator, a lender, a business owner, or someone building technology.

There isn't one ladder.

There are dozens.

Yet people often follow the career path of whoever appears most successful around them without considering whether they actually want that person's life.

You admire a brokerage owner with two hundred agents.

Wonderful.

Would you like to manage two hundred agents?

You admire a founder who has raised millions.

Would you enjoy the obligations that come with investors, payroll, and growth targets?

You admire someone with a huge social media audience.

Would you want the content schedule required to maintain it?

A person's success can be real and impressive without being a suitable blueprint for your own life.

I think that's something we should ask ourselves more often.

Not just, *Do I want what they have?*

But, *Would I want their calendar? And am I willing to pay the price they're paying?*

Because every version of success comes with obligations.

Time.

Responsibility.

Financial risk.

The things you have to give up to make room for the things you've chosen.

And the price isn't always visible from the outside.

If the answer is no, copying someone else's strategy may be a very expensive way to reach a destination you never wanted.

## I want the freedom to pack a bag

Here's what an amazing life looks like to me.

I want to be able to pack a bag and spend three months in Paris. Or Porto. Or Barcelona.

Not because I've escaped my responsibilities or because I'm trying to convince the internet that my life is one long European vacation.

I want to wake up somewhere different, walk to a little café, spend an afternoon exploring a city, argue with my wife about why the rat in the corner is obviously Ratatouille, and still have meaningful work to do when I feel like doing it.

I want the ability to choose.

And I want to build a life where that choice doesn't require an elaborate financial negotiation with myself every time I consider it.

Maybe your version looks completely different.

I don't presume to know what you want. I can only speak from personal experience, and from years of watching people pursue things they believed would make them happy.

I've learned that the people who seem to have it all often don't have nearly as much freedom as their lives suggest.

Sometimes what looks like wealth is an enormous financial obligation wearing beautiful clothes.

Sometimes what looks like success is a calendar that belongs to everybody except the person whose name is on it.

And sometimes the dream lifestyle comes with a monthly payment that quietly takes away the freedom it was supposed to represent.

I would hate to see you finance your dream life at 30% interest just to convince somebody else you've arrived.

There's nothing wrong with wanting beautiful things. I want beautiful things, too.

But don't lose yourself, or everything you've worked so hard to build, because the optics on the other side look shiny.

The view may be spectacular.

The price of maintaining it might not be.

I'd rather build a life that gives me the freedom to leave for Paris than one that requires me to stay home to pay for the appearance of having that freedom.

## I'm not finished building

There's one more opinion I have that may be less controversial than it once was.

I don't think ambition has an expiration date.

I'm in my forties. I've spent more than twenty years working in real estate, and this is the first time I'm seriously putting my thoughts and experience into public view.

I'm learning how to create content.

I'm building software.

I'm exploring ideas that may work beautifully or become further additions to my extensive collection of educational experiences.

And I'm enjoying the process of becoming a beginner again, even when I'd rather not hear my own recorded voice.

I don't think there's anything contradictory about being grateful for your life and wanting to build another layer on top of it.

The top of one mountain is often the beginning of another.

That's perfectly fine with me.

I just want to enjoy the climb instead of spending the entire journey worrying about what the view will look like to somebody else.

I don't particularly want to retire in the traditional sense.

But I also don't want to work eighty hours a week just to maintain a lifestyle that leaves me no time to enjoy it.

I want autonomy.

I want options.

I want to work with capable, interesting people and pursue projects because they matter to me, not because every decision has to produce an immediate paycheck.

I want to keep building, keep learning, and enjoy the climb without becoming a prisoner of everything I've accumulated along the way.

So when I share my two cents about real estate, business, money, technology, or the occasional absurdity of professional life, understand that they come through a particular lens.

A Cuban girl raised by a hardworking single mother. A woman who spent decades learning how businesses operate from behind the scenes. Someone who loves her family, enjoys building things, and wants the freedom to pack a bag and spend three months somewhere beautiful.

I don't have every answer, and I reserve the right to change my mind when I find a better one.

I'm simply sharing what life has taught me so far.

**This is my lens. Your view may be different. And that's what makes the conversation interesting.**
