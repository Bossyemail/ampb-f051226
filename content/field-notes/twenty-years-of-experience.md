I don't care whether you've had your real estate license for twenty minutes or twenty years. You deserve to be treated with professional respect.

That's the minimum. It shouldn't require a credential check.

I've worked with newer agents who were organized, diligent, punctual, and remarkably good at recognizing what they didn't know. They asked questions, followed through, paid attention, and took their responsibilities seriously.

I've also worked with experienced agents who made an otherwise ordinary transaction feel like a competitive endurance event.

Which raises a question I've been thinking about for some time: when did years in the industry become an acceptable substitute for professional behavior?

Experience matters. Of course it does. I've been working in South Florida real estate since 2004, and I know that time spent doing this work can develop judgment you won't find in a textbook.

But experience is only useful when something has been learned from it.

Twenty years in a business doesn't automatically mean twenty years of development. Sometimes it means repeating the same habits for twenty years, with increasing confidence that nobody should question them.

## The Industry Has Changed. Your Anniversary Hasn't Stopped It.

Every industry has people who remember how things used to be done. Real estate has a particularly dedicated collection of them.

You know the conversation.

*"I've been doing this since before you were born."*

Congratulations. That still doesn't answer the question about the missing document.

Or my personal favorite:

*"That's not how we've ever done it."*

That might be true. It might also be completely irrelevant.

Contracts evolve. Disclosure requirements change. Brokerage procedures change. Technology changes. What was standard practice ten years ago may no longer be appropriate today.

When a new requirement takes effect, pointing to your license anniversary doesn't make it disappear.

I didn't invent the requirement, Janet. I'm just asking for the paperwork.

There's nothing wrong with preferring familiar processes or questioning whether a new procedure is useful. There is a problem when refusing to adapt becomes part of your professional identity.

Because other people still have to finish the transaction.

## Confidence and Competence Aren't the Same Thing

I have considerable respect for people who can say, "I'm not familiar with that. Can you explain it?"

That sentence saves time.

It creates an opportunity to clarify the issue, identify the correct process, and move forward.

Compare that with the person who responds to every unfamiliar request as though their professional reputation has been personally attacked. Suddenly, what could have been a two-minute clarification requires multiple emails, phone calls, and an audience.

All because somebody needed a document.

One of the patterns I've noticed is that some newer professionals are more comfortable asking questions precisely because they understand they're still learning. They don't necessarily feel pressure to perform expertise they haven't acquired.

Some experienced professionals, on the other hand, seem to believe acknowledging a gap in their knowledge would undermine everything they've accomplished.

I think the opposite is true.

The longer you've worked in a field, the more comfortable you should become with the fact that you will never know everything.

That's not a threat to your expertise. It's part of maintaining it.

## Professionalism Isn't a Seniority Benefit

Respect should work in both directions.

A new agent doesn't deserve to be dismissed because they're new. A veteran doesn't deserve to be treated poorly simply because their methods seem outdated. Both deserve straightforward communication, accurate information, and a reasonable opportunity to do their jobs.

But nobody earns the right to make everybody else's work unnecessarily difficult.

Experience may explain why someone has certain expectations. It doesn't excuse refusing to respond, withholding information, talking down to colleagues, or turning routine requests into arguments about who has been in the business longer.

And professionalism isn't measured by how often you remind other people of your accomplishments.

It's visible in how you handle questions, corrections, disagreements, and situations where you don't have an immediate answer.

After years of managing transactions, I've come to value something more than an impressive professional biography: the ability to work through a problem without creating three new ones.

Give me the newer agent who takes responsibility, follows through, and wants to learn over the seasoned professional who believes their tenure entitles them to make everyone miserable.

Every time.

Because the number of years you've been working tells me how long you've been here.

How you work tells me what you've learned.
