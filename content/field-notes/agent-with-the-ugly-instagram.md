*Somewhere in South Florida, an agent with a blurry profile picture, a questionable font, and twelve posts from 2019 is having a better year than someone with an entire content production team.*

I know. It's upsetting.

Especially if you've spent the last three hours deciding whether your Instagram grid should be beige, greige, or whatever we're calling that color that looks like oatmeal having a difficult morning.

Meanwhile, there's an agent whose last post was a slightly crooked photograph of a closing table. The caption says, "Congratulations to my buyers!!!" Seventeen exclamation points. Three different fonts. Possibly a watermark from an app that no longer exists.

And the phone keeps ringing.

Not because Instagram secretly rewards bad graphic design.

Because people know this person.

## The business that happens when nobody is watching

Before social media, agents built their businesses through relationships, referrals, reputation, and being the person someone thought to call when real estate came up.

Plenty of agents still operate that way.

They know their neighborhoods. They remember the people they worked with five years ago. They answer when someone calls. They know which former client just became a grandparent and which family might be ready to move closer to their children.

They show up to community events without requiring a photographer.

Imagine that.

I know a lender who built his career doing the kinds of deals most lenders won't even touch. The complicated ones. The ones that require someone who actually understands how to get a file through underwriting.

And he can get them closed in two weeks.

No social media. None.

No reels explaining the five mistakes buyers make. No cinematic footage of him walking into the office holding a coffee. No motivational captions about crushing another Monday.

Just a reputation for getting difficult deals done.

People call him because they know what he can do.

Some agents have built the same kind of business. They've helped siblings buy homes, watched families grow, sold properties for parents, and later helped the children purchase their first homes.

That's not an Instagram strategy.

That's a career.

And while they're not necessarily attracting thousands of new followers every month, they're building something that doesn't reset every time an algorithm changes.

Trust accumulated over years.

You can't download that from Canva.

## The problem with looking successful

I work with real estate agents. I've watched people build extraordinary businesses, and I've watched others spend extraordinary amounts of time making their businesses look extraordinary.

Those are not always the same activity.

A beautiful profile can make someone curious about you. Good video can demonstrate expertise. Useful content can introduce you to people you would never otherwise meet.

I'm doing it myself. I'm writing this for a website, after all. I understand the irony.

But somewhere along the way, we started treating visibility as evidence of success.

Ten thousand followers. Perfect headshots. Coordinated reels. A personal brand that looks like it has a creative director, three assistants, and a standing reservation at a rooftop restaurant.

None of those things tells me whether someone will return a client's phone call.

Or follow up after a showing.

Or remember the name of the person who referred them.

And those are the things people tend to remember long after they've forgotten your beautifully edited property tour.

The most impressive profile in the world can't make somebody trust you after you've disappointed them.

## Some agents are playing a different game

The agent with the ugly Instagram may not be trying to become an influencer.

They might not want a national audience. They might not care whether someone in California thinks their branding is sophisticated.

They want the person who lives six blocks away to call them when it's time to sell.

They want the referral from an attorney they've known for fifteen years.

They want a former client to say, "Call her. She took care of us."

And if that happens consistently, the fact that their profile picture looks like it was taken on a flip phone is probably not keeping them awake at night.

They already have distribution.

It's called people.

Of course, not every agent with an outdated Instagram has a thriving business. Some just have an outdated Instagram.

But the successful ones remind us that there's more than one way to build demand, and not all of it happens publicly.

## The part nobody can fake for very long

You can hire someone to build your brand.

You can pay someone to edit your videos, write captions, schedule posts, and make your business look polished.

There is nothing wrong with any of that. In fact, good marketing can be a meaningful advantage.

What I find fascinating is the number of people creating content to collect views without asking whether any of those views could become clients.

A hundred thousand views from people who will never buy, sell, or refer a property to you might be wonderful for your ego.

But if your goal is to generate business, shouldn't the content be designed to reach people who might actually do business with you?

Creating content for views without thinking about the customer you're trying to attract is a fascinating business strategy.

Unless views are the business.

In which case, carry on.

And don't get me started on the "just post more" crowd.

Of course you can post three times a day when you have a content strategist, a videographer, two editors, a social media manager, and four other people whose job descriptions I don't entirely understand.

Meanwhile, you're sitting there with you, yourself, and two AI tools that are probably talking about you behind your back.

And somehow you're expected to run a full-time business, serve your clients, generate new leads, learn video editing, understand the algorithm, and produce something worth watching before lunch.

It's possible.

It's also work.

A lot of it.

## So should you stop posting?

Absolutely not.

But understand that social media isn't a silver bullet for an empty pipeline.

Creating content, especially good content, is an underrated skill. And the people who make it look effortless have often spent years getting very good at something the rest of us are trying to learn between client calls.

Consider a simple two-minute video.

First, you need something worth saying. Then you need to figure out how to say it in a way that makes someone want to listen.

There's the delivery. The filming. The editing. The captions. The lighting. Learning to look into a camera without appearing to have been taken hostage.

Then you hear your recorded voice.

An experience that should probably come with a warning label.

You remove the awkward silences because apparently we're no longer allowed to pause for emphasis without losing half the internet.

You watch the video fourteen times, decide your face looks strange in the third second, fix the captions, change the opening, and consider abandoning the entire project to become a person who sells candles.

Meanwhile, you've ignored three phone calls and ten notifications because you're trying to finish the thing.

Finally, you post.

Ten views.

One like.

And a comment from a suspicious account saying, "Send me this post."

As if.

Now, perhaps I'm saying this because I haven't had enough coffee, but I think we underestimate how difficult it is to become genuinely good at creating content while also being responsible for an actual business.

The first videos may be terrible. The next twenty might be slightly less terrible. You learn how to speak, how to structure an idea, how to edit without losing your mind, and eventually how to recognize what your audience finds valuable.

That takes repetition.

Sometimes hundreds of videos.

And it can be discouraging when you're learning in public while everyone else appears to have arrived fully formed, professionally lit, and inexplicably well rested.

A lot of people quit before they've given themselves enough time to develop the skill, let alone see a return on it.

Especially when they also have clients, bills, families, responsibilities, and the audacity to want a life outside their business.

## Learn from the people getting results

There are agents generating real business through social media.

Absolutely.

Some have built remarkable audiences that consistently turn into inquiries, referrals, appointments, and sales.

Those are the people worth studying if that is your goal.

Not simply because their videos went viral, but because they've figured out how to connect attention to a business outcome.

Study what they talk about. Who they speak to. How they build trust. What happens after someone follows them. Whether their content answers questions potential clients actually have.

And pay attention to their resources, too.

A content operation supported by ten people is not a reasonable daily benchmark for someone producing videos alone between transactions.

Take the principles that work. Adapt the execution to the business and capacity you actually have.

You don't have to become a media company to be a good real estate agent.

And you don't have to reject social media to build a strong referral business.

You can do both. Just don't confuse one with the other.

Because somewhere in South Florida, the agent with the crooked closing photo and seventeen exclamation points just got another referral.

She probably hasn't checked her engagement rate.

She's busy answering the phone.
