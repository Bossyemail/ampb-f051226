*Twenty-two years in South Florida real estate taught me that the people who look the most successful aren't always the people who know how to get a deal closed.*

I was 18 years old when I started working in real estate, surrounded by professionals in their forties who seemed to have everything I didn't. Experience. Connections. Authority. Offices with doors.

Being young was rarely an advantage.

Clients wanted someone older. Someone senior. Someone with enough influence to make things happen. And since I couldn't manufacture another twenty years of experience, I decided to become the person people needed when they wanted something done.

Eventually, I became the only person in an office of roughly 600 with a direct line to the underwriter.

Was I young? Absolutely.

Was I going to let that stop me? Cry me a river.

Back then, there was no Instagram to help you look important. No professionally edited videos announcing that you were crushing it. No personal branding consultant to explain which shade of beige communicated authority.

You had a reputation, a production record, and people who could tell you apart from someone who was just very good at talking.

It wasn't a perfect industry. Far from it. But it was considerably harder to confuse visibility with competence.

Today, I watch agents spend extraordinary amounts of energy managing the appearance of their business. The photography is beautiful. The branding is immaculate. The Instagram profile looks like it belongs to a luxury resort in the Maldives.

And then someone asks for the contract's effective date.

Silence.

Apparently, that didn't make it into the brand guidelines.

## The part of the business nobody photographs

I've worked across lending, title, operations, contracts and transaction management. After more than 3,000 closings, I've had a front-row seat to what happens after a property goes under contract.

That's where my understanding of real estate sales changed.

Because getting a contract signed is one accomplishment. Getting everyone to the closing table is another.

And the second part has very little interest in how successful you look.

It requires people to read things. Respond to questions. Make decisions. Follow up. Recognize problems before they become emergencies. Know when something requires the attention of a broker, lender, attorney or title company.

A surprising amount of professional competence consists of doing very ordinary things consistently well.

The best agents I've worked with aren't necessarily the loudest, the busiest or the ones with the most impressive online presence.

They're the ones who know what's happening in their transactions.

They ask questions before deadlines. They understand what they've agreed to. They don't disappear the moment a problem becomes inconvenient.

And when they don't know something, they ask.

Which is considerably more reassuring than someone who has been doing this for twenty years and considers reading the contract an optional activity.

Experience should make you more careful, not more casual.

## The client isn't in the room

Here's something I wish more sellers understood.

When you hire an agent, you're trusting that person to represent you in conversations you'll probably never hear.

With the buyer's agent. The lender. The title company. The attorney. The association. The people trying to solve whatever happened between the contract being signed and the closing date.

And I've read emails that made me stop and think: *Would you say that to your client?*

Would you use that tone?

Would you ignore that question?

Would you make that accusation without first checking whether you were right?

Because if the answer is no, I have a follow-up question.

Why is it acceptable to say it to the person on the other side of the transaction?

Come on now.

Professionalism isn't a performance reserved for the people paying you. It's how you behave when the people paying you aren't watching.

Your clients might never know that someone spent three days trying to reach you. They might never see the unnecessary argument you started, the information you failed to provide, or the number of people who had to work around you to keep the transaction moving.

On a good day, everyone involved quietly does the heavy lifting, the closing happens, and the client goes home happy.

On a bad day, a solvable problem becomes an expensive one because someone was unavailable when a decision needed to be made.

Sometimes the agent finally resurfaces at 8 p.m., fresh from a productive afternoon filming content, wondering why nobody is answering.

Well, Janet, the rest of the transaction also contains human beings. Some of them have gone home.

## Three thousand transactions start looking like patterns

One difficult transaction can feel like bad luck.

After 3,000, certain things stop looking accidental.

Most deals don't get into trouble because of one enormous mistake. They get there through a series of smaller ones.

An assumption nobody checked. A deposit someone thought had been received. A document everyone believed the other person was handling. A question that went unanswered for two days. A deadline that seemed far away until suddenly it wasn't.

The contract is often sitting there doing exactly what it was written to do.

The chaos comes from how people handle it.

I've learned that *I thought* is one of the most expensive phrases in this business.

"I thought title handled it."

"I thought the buyer knew."

"I thought the lender had everything."

"I thought someone followed up."

And sometimes, everybody thought.

Nobody confirmed.

Risk lives in that gap.

This is why I have a particular appreciation for people who document things, calendar deadlines, confirm information and follow up without being chased.

It's not glamorous. Nobody has ever built a personal brand around confirming receipt of an escrow deposit.

Perhaps they should.

Because a good agent doesn't prevent every problem. That would be impossible.

A good agent prevents one problem from becoming five.

## The people doing the work are often invisible

There's another pattern I've noticed.

When a transaction closes smoothly, people tend to remember the person who sold them the property.

Fair enough. That's an important relationship.

But they rarely see the lender who spent three days resolving a condition, the title officer who caught a discrepancy, the assistant who followed up six times, or the transaction manager who recognized that something was about to become a problem.

And that's how a successful closing should feel to the client.

The work is supposed to produce a reliable outcome, not a dramatic story about everything that nearly went wrong.

The problem begins when the people benefiting from that work forget it exists.

I've met agents who understand that a transaction is a collection of professionals working toward the same result.

I've also met agents who treat everyone around them as a supporting character in the extraordinary motion picture of their own success.

The first group tends to build relationships people want to maintain.

The second group eventually discovers that being technically successful doesn't necessarily make you pleasant—or easy—to do business with.

And the people on the other side of the transaction remember.

They remember who answered.

Who followed through.

Who took responsibility.

Who made the work unnecessarily difficult.

Your reputation is being built in conversations you aren't part of.

## What I respect now

At 18, I thought authority came from being the most important person in the room.

Or at least from becoming important enough that people would stop treating my age like a professional disability.

Twenty-two years later, my definition has changed.

I respect the person who knows what they're doing and doesn't need everyone to know it.

The professional who can explain a complicated situation without making the client feel stupid. The agent who acknowledges a mistake and corrects it. The person who asks a question instead of pretending to know the answer.

I respect people who understand that their responsibilities don't end where someone else's job begins.

And I have enormous respect for people who make difficult work look ordinary through preparation and follow-through.

Not because they never have problems.

Because they know what to do when problems appear.

Social media has made it easier than ever to look established, successful and in demand. There are talented agents who use it brilliantly, and I have no objection to good marketing.

But a beautiful brand can't negotiate an extension, explain a contract or make an unanswered email disappear.

And no amount of production value changes what happens when somebody needs you to make a decision.

After 3,000 closings, I've become much less interested in who looks important.

I'm interested in who understands the assignment.

Because one deal can feel random.

Three thousand deals start looking like patterns.

And the people who consistently get good outcomes tend to have something in common.

They do the work when nobody is watching.
